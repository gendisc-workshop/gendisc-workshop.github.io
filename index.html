Workshop scope

Generative models are opening new avenues for discovery across the experimental sciences. RFdiffusion has produced experimentally validated protein binders; DISCO has designed heme enzymes that catalyse new-to-nature reactions; and AMP-Diffusion has generated antimicrobial peptides that reduced drug-resistant skin infections in mice. Beyond molecular design, MatterGen guided the synthesis of a material with controlled stiffness, while diffusion priors enabled exploration of alternative black hole reconstructions consistent with sparse telescope measurements.

A generative model is not itself a discovery system: matching the distribution of known structures and sampling from it does not determine how to explore promising regions, adapt from feedback, or choose informative experiments. Valuable designs may lie beyond a model’s current reach, while their validity and utility may only be revealed through uncertain and costly feedback.

Generative discovery is an emerging area of machine learning at the intersection of generative modelling, sequential decision-making, and optimisation, centred on a common question: How should generative models explore, adapt, and learn from experiments to enable discovery in the experimental sciences?

Addressing this question raises open problems in generative discovery foundations, methods, systems, and evaluation metrics. The following are representative examples of open problems across four broad themes:

  • Objectives and formulations. What should a discovery system optimise when success depends on novelty, diversity, feasibility, and scientific value? How should these objectives be formalised beyond likelihood or average reward?
  • Exploration with generative models. How can models explore valid, valuable regions underrepresented or absent in their training data? When should a system optimise within its current reach, and when should it expand what it can generate?
  • Learning from data and feedback. How can generators learn from incomplete data and scarce, uncertain, or costly feedback? How should they quantify uncertainty, select informative experiments, and adapt efficiently across sources of differing fidelity?
  • Definition and validation of discoveries. How should progress be measured and discoveries validated? How can we distinguish novelty to the model from novelty to science, and align computational metrics to experimental outcomes?

GenDisc focuses on foundational and methodological questions, using applications in experimental sciences to test conceptual assumptions, expose algorithmic limitations, and ultimately shape theoretical and methodological frameworks for real-world validation and scientific impact.

Call for papers

Draft call · submissions not open

GenDisc focuses on the questions that arise when generative models are used to discover scientifically valuable structures, designs, and hypotheses. We welcome work across the following themes. The sub-topics listed below are illustrative rather than exhaustive.

Topics of interest

Theory and foundations

  • Formalisations of novelty, surprise, creativity, scientific value, and generative reach.
  • Objectives beyond expected reward, including rare event generation, risk-sensitive control, and multi-objective formulations.
  • Optimisation theory for discovery-oriented generative objectives.
  • Out-of-distribution generative modelling and coverage beyond the training distribution.
  • Theory of exploration and open-endedness in generative models.
  • Limits on the sample, computational, and experimental complexity of discovery.

Methods and systems

  • Generative optimisation and inverse design via generative priors.
  • Algorithms that steer, compose, or fine-tune pretrained generators for discovery.
  • Exploration and diverse sample generation via generative model steering.
  • Rare event generation and distributional control of generative models.
  • Sample-efficient generative model steering and fine-tuning.
  • Adapting generative models via experimental, approximate, or multi-fidelity feedback.
  • Sim-to-real gap, or test-time distribution shift between computational and real experiments.
  • Out-of-distribution generative modelling for discovery.
  • Active and cost-aware selection of informative experiments via generative models.
  • Generative models within closed-loop discovery systems.

Computational or experimental validation

  • Prospective experimental or high-fidelity validation of generated candidates.
  • Benchmarks for novelty, diversity, validity, utility, and efficiency.
  • Validation of generated experiments and scientific hypotheses.
  • Evaluation under noisy measurements, imperfect oracles, and feasibility constraints.
  • Replication, reproducibility, and robustness of discovery claims.
  • Positive or negative results in any application that reveal insights, strengths, or limitations of current generative discovery methods.

This workshop is agnostic to the choice of generative model. Relevant approaches include diffusion and flow models, autoregressive and discrete generative models, GFlowNets, energy-based and latent-variable models, and evolutionary or hybrid generative search; related approaches are equally welcome. Scientific domains may provide motivating problems and evidence, but contributions should address questions or principles of generative discovery that might extend beyond a single application.

Out of scope: pure prediction; unconditional generation evaluated only by sample quality; general-purpose generative modelling or post-training without a discovery-related objective; and application-specific work whose primary contribution is a result in one scientific domain rather than a broader methodological insight about generative discovery that might transfer across domains.

Submission formats

FormatLimitContribution
Full paper9 pagesOriginal contributions addressing the workshop themes.
Short paper4 pagesPreliminary results, focused observations, and recent findings.
Tiny paper2 pagesSimple ideas with initial evaluation, modest theoretical results, re-analyses, or new perspectives, particularly from authors outside the usual ML publication circuit.

References and appendices are excluded from page limits. Perspectives, benchmarks, and negative results are welcome in every format.

Review and presentation

  • Double-blind, non-archival OpenReview process. We will prioritise ongoing and novel work; accepted papers may be under review or subsequently published elsewhere, subject to the policies of other venues.
  • Each submission will receive at least two reviews. Reviewer assignment will reflect the nature of the contribution rather than a fixed track, with complementary expertise sought for work spanning theory, methods, and scientific validation.
  • All accepted papers will be presented as posters, with selected papers receiving contributed talks: nine presentations of 10 minutes each, three for each workshop theme.

Under the proposed guidelines, short and tiny papers must be primarily human-authored. AI assistance is permitted; AI-generated papers are not.

The workshop is proposed for ICLR 2027. Final instructions and the submission link will be published after acceptance.

Panel

Bridging the Gaps in Generative Discovery

A proposed 60-minute panel bridging theoretical formulations and experimental needs, and scientific domains that face related discovery problems.

Module 01

Bridging theory and experiment

Panellists confront theoretical principles with experimental constraints, examining where current methods serve scientific practice and where they still fall short. The discussion connects theoretical guarantees, computational benchmarks, and experimental validation with advances in generators, generative Bayesian optimisation, inverse modelling, and experimental design.

Module 02

Bridging diverse scientific domains

Panellists compare hidden assumptions and scientific priorities across domains, testing which methodological approaches to generative discovery transfer across applications. Topics include simulation-first discovery and the meanings of diversity, novelty, and discovery across the sciences.
